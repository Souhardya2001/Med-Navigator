Title: Type 2 Diabetes

Overview
Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the body does not use insulin effectively and, over time, may not produce enough insulin to maintain normal blood glucose levels. Persistent elevation of blood glucose can affect blood vessels, nerves, kidneys, eyes, and other organs.

Risk Factors
Important risk factors include excess body weight, physical inactivity, family history of diabetes, increasing age, and a history of certain metabolic conditions. However, type 2 diabetes can develop in people without all of these risk factors.

Common Symptoms
Some people have no obvious symptoms, especially during the early stages. Possible symptoms include increased thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, unexplained weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, and slow healing of wounds. Recurrent infections may also occur.

Diagnosis
Healthcare professionals can use blood tests such as fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin (A1C), or an oral glucose tolerance test to evaluate blood glucose levels. The appropriate test and interpretation depend on the clinical situation.

Management
Management commonly involves a balanced eating pattern, regular physical activity, weight management when appropriate, monitoring blood glucose when advised, and medications when prescribed. Metformin and several other medication classes may be used depending on an individual's health status and treatment goals.

Long-Term Health
Good glucose management can reduce the risk of diabetes-related complications. Regular monitoring may include assessment of blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney function, eye health, and foot health.

Important Note
Diabetes treatment should be individualized by a healthcare professional. Symptoms such as severe weakness, confusion, vomiting, difficulty breathing, or loss of consciousness require urgent medical attention.
